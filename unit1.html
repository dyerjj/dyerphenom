Blog: A phenomenon in My world

My specific and original phenomenon is how illness in college leads to use of over-the-counter medication, and how instead of choosing healthier habits or trying to find the root of the problem, students tend to look for temporary solutions. I selected this one because it was the one I found that I could relate to the most and that I saw the most around me.

My current understanding of the phenomenon is how normalized it is to suppress symptoms with medications and various drugs. One of my inquiries is that this could potentially be harmful depending on the user. As new college students, this could be the first time overseeing their own medication and this can easily lead to drug abuse. This suggests my phenomenon because students then start to overuse painkillers, sleep medications, or any kind of over-the-counter drug.

I assume students choose OTC medication because it is easier and faster than addressing root problems, rather than limited access to healthcare or time constraints. For the “so what” I asked why this pattern matters beyond individual health. I think it is a societal norm thrown onto young adults, making them think it is normal to prescribe amounts to themselves. For the “how” I asked how the shift from parent supervision to managing themselves and their medication. I want to observe more details of this change.

For the “why” I asked why students' default to symptom suppression does instead of seeking the root cause. The three popular culture artifacts I selected are two articles on “the frat flu”, a medication Calander comic, and a comic from New York Cartoons. I think my strongest cartoon is the medication Calander because it includes Excedrin. I think it will expand my current interpretation because Excedrin is a medication that relieves migraines, a symptom that most students experience.

While college students' use of over-the-counter medication can be seen as a practical response to individual illness (X), examining pop culture artifacts like “frat flu” narratives and medication-themed comics suggests that this behavior may also be a symptom of a broader cultural norm that pressures young adults into self-managing their health before they're equipped to do so (Y).
